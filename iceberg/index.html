AI's reach into work is mostly underwater.

A US study modelled 151 million workers against 13,000 AI tools. The AI everyone talks about turned out to be the tip.

In shortThe 30-second version

Three things to take away.

01

The AI you hear about is the small part.

Coding assistants and tech layoffs make the news. That visible activity covers about 2% of what Americans are paid to do.

02

The bigger part is ordinary office work.

AI tools can already perform tasks worth nearly 12% of wages, mostly in administration, finance, HR and customer support. Five times the visible part.

03

It is about tasks, and nobody's dashboard shows it.

The study measures what AI can do inside a job, not jobs lost. Headcount, budget and economic reports will pick it up last.

The study covers the United States only. No Australian figures are given.

Inside a jobTasks, not job titles

No job on the list disappears. Parts of it move.

Every role is a bundle of tasks. The Index measures how much of that bundle AI tools can already handle. Three examples the report itself uses:

Financial analyst

The role stays. How it is structured changes, and so do the skills in demand, without necessarily cutting headcount.

More time for direct patient care. The report sees this easing staff shortages while improving quality.

The job shifts towards oversight, testing and fitting systems together.

Why nowThe measurement gap

Official statistics count what already happened.

Employment and GDP figures record disruption after the fact, and they count jobs tied to places. Work done alongside AI has no postcode and no job category, so it barely registers. Meanwhile the shift is under way.

The measureWhat the Index is

A map of what AI can do. Not a forecast of who loses a job.

For every occupation, the Iceberg Index asks one question: what share of this job's wage value sits in skills that AI tools can already perform?

It measures

Technical exposure

  • Where AI capability and human skills overlap
  • Before adoption shows up in the data
  • The same way in every state and industry

It does not predict

Job losses

  • How many people will be displaced
  • How fast employers will adopt AI
  • The net effect on employment

Read it like an earthquake hazard map. It shows where the ground could move, not when it will.

A financial analyst's job does not vanish. But a large part of the document processing and routine analysis inside it is work AI has shown it can do, and that changes how the role is built.

The modelHow it was built

A digital twin of the American workforce.

Each worker becomes a software agent with skills, tasks and a location. Each AI tool is tagged with the skills it can perform. Skills are the shared currency: where both sides land on the same one, that is exposure.

    Back at the surfaceFor managers

    What to do with this if you lead a team.

    The report is written for US state governments. These five points are our translation of its findings for anyone running a team or an organisation.

    Look past the IT team.

    If AI planning lives only with the technical staff, it covers the tip. The larger exposure is in the office functions around them.

    AskWho is looking at AI in finance, HR, admin and support?

    Think in tasks, not job titles.

    Exposure sits in tasks like document processing, routine analysis and coordination. Most roles contain some of each.

    AskWhat does each role actually do, week to week?

    Decide where the time goes.

    The report's best case is paperwork handled by AI so skilled people spend more time on the work only they can do.

    AskIf a task took half as long, what would we do with the hours?

    Know your shape.

    Exposure packed into one or two functions calls for a targeted plan. Exposure spread thinly across everyone calls for coordination.

    AskIs ours concentrated or spread out?

    Do not wait for the numbers.

    The usual indicators explained less than 5% of the hidden exposure. By the time it shows in the reports, the chance to prepare has gone.

    AskWhat could we test now, on a small scale, before we have to?

    Fine printRead it carefully

    What the authors say it cannot tell you.

    01

    Exposure is not displacement

    Real outcomes depend on what firms adopt, how workers adapt, regulation and public acceptance.

    02

    It is an upper bound

    The Index assumes a skill AI shows in one job carries over to others. Near-term exposure may be lower.

    03

    Digital only

    Robotics and physical automation are left out until adoption data matures.

    04

    Wages flatten the detail

    Weighting by pay hides differences inside a job and says nothing about job quality.

    05

    Checked, not proven

    The validation is correlation against today's patterns. It does not show exposure causes outcomes.

    06

    A companion, not a replacement

    GDP and unemployment track what has happened. The Index shows what could. Planning needs both.
